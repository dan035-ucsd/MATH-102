\subsection{Lecture 2 - Introduction to PDEs}
\underline{Conservation Laws} \\
This is used to describe: bacterial movement in a petridish, nerve signal traveling 
through an axon, heat diffusion in a rod, cars on the highway. \\ \\ 
We start with the following assumptions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Motion happens in 1D.
    \item Cross-sectional area is constant.
\end{itemize}
Consider a 1D tube, with particles inside. How do we describe the change of 
concentration of particles?
\begin{align*}
    \begin{matrix}
    \text{change in pop between } \\ x \text{ and } x + \Delta x
    \end{matrix} = 
    \begin{matrix}
        \text{rate of entry into} \\ (x,x+\Delta x) \\ \text{per unit time}
    \end{matrix} - 
    \begin{matrix}
        \text{rate of exit into} \\ (x,x+\Delta x) \\ \text{per unit time}
    \end{matrix}
\end{align*}
\underline{Define}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $c(x,t) = $ concentration of particles at $x$ at time $t$
    \item $J(x,t) = $ flux of particles at $(x,t) = $ \# of particles
    crossing a unit area at $x$ in the positive direction, per unit time.
    \item $A = $ cross-sectional area of the tube.
    \item $\Delta V = $ volume of our segment = $A\cdot \Delta x$  
\end{itemize}
We have 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\underbrace{c(x,t)\cdot A\cdot \Delta x}_{\text{\# of particles}})
    &= J(x,t)A - J(x+\Delta x,t)A \\
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= \frac{J(x,t) - J(x+\Delta x, t)}{\Delta x} \\
    \lim_{\Delta x \rightarrow 0} \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= \lim_{\Delta x \rightarrow 0}\frac{J(x,t) - J(x+\Delta x, t)}{\Delta x} \\
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\ \leftarrow \text{Conservation of mass}
\end{align*}
Consider a new term,
\begin{align*}
    \begin{matrix}
    \Delta \text{pop between } \\ x \text{ and } x + \Delta x
    \end{matrix} = 
    \begin{matrix}
        \text{rate of entry into} \\ (x,x+\Delta x) \\ \text{per unit time}
    \end{matrix} - 
    \begin{matrix}
        \text{rate of exit into} \\ (x,x+\Delta x) \\ \text{per unit time}
    \end{matrix}
    \pm \begin{matrix}
        \text{rate of local} \\ \text{creation} \\ \text{or degradation}
    \end{matrix}
\end{align*}
We now have
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\sigma(x,t) = $ \# of particles created/removed per unit volume at time $t$   
\end{itemize}
Now our conservation laws become
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} \pm \sigma(x,t)
\end{align*}
To generalize to 2D or 3D:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= -\vec{\nabla} \vec{J} \pm \sigma(\vec x, t) \\
    \text{Now we have } c &= c(\vec{x}, t) \\
    \sigma &= \sigma(\vec{x}, t) \\
    \vec{J} &= (J_x, J_y, J_z) \\
    \vec{\nabla} &= (\frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z})
\end{align*}
So we have 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = - (\frac{\partial J_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial J_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial J_z}{\partial z}) \pm \sigma (x,y,z,t)
\end{align*}
\boxed{\text{ex}} Consider a moving fluid + organisms that move with the flow (not actively). 
Suppose that the velocity of the fluid is given by 
\begin{align*}
    \vec{v}(x,y,z,t)
\end{align*}
Let $c(x,y,z,t) = $ concentration of organism. \\ \\
What is the flux? 
\begin{align*}
    \vec{J} &= c\cdot \vec{V} \quad \text{Assume } \sigma = 0 \\
    \Rightarrow \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= - \left(\frac{\partial cV_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial cV_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial cV_z}{\partial z}\right) 
\end{align*}
In 1D: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= - \frac{\partial}{\partial x} (cV) \\
    &= - \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} V - \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} c
\end{align*}
What kind of PDE is this?
\begin{itemize}
    \item 1st order
    \item 2 variables: $x,t$
    \item Linear
    \item Transport equation
\end{itemize}
\boxed{\text{ex}} Attraction/repulsion. Suppose that $\psi$ is a source of attraction for the
bacteria. The motion is determined by the gradient of $\psi$. \\
In 1D: $\psi = \psi(x,t),\ J = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$ 
\begin{align*}
    J = c\alpha\vec{\nabla}\psi
\end{align*}
We have 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= - \frac{\partial J}{\partial X} \\
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= - \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left(c\alpha \frac{\partial \psi}{\psi x}\right)
\end{align*}
$\psi$ is given.
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = - \alpha \frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\cdot \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} - \alpha c \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}
\end{align*}
This is 1st order, linear PDE in 2 variables. \\ \\
\boxed{\text{ex}} Temperature of a heated rod. \\
Here we define $c(x,t)$ as concentration of energy/temperature. And since heat spreads
from hot to cold, we get the following.
\begin{center}
    \boxed{\text{Fick's Law}: J = -D \cdot \frac{\partial c}{\partial x}}
\end{center}
Then we have
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= - \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( -D \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \right)\\
    &= D \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}\ \leftarrow \text{Heat equation}
\end{align*}
In higher dimensions: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= \vec{\nabla}(D\vec{\nabla}c) \\
    &= D \left( \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial z^2}\right)
\end{align*}
\boxed{\text{ex}} Random motion of particles. \\
Denote $\lambda_r$ as the amount of particles jumping from $x \rightarrow x+\Delta x$ \\
Denote $\lambda_l$ as the amount of particles jumping from $x \rightarrow x-\Delta x$ \\
Denote $c(x,t) = $ \# of particles within $(x,x+\Delta x)$ at time $t$: 
\begin{align*}
    c(x,t+\Delta t) = c(x,t) + \lambda_r\cdot c(x-\Delta x,t) + \lambda_l \cdot c(x+\Delta x,t) - \lambda_lc(x,t) - \lambda_rc(x,t)
\end{align*}
Using Taylor Series:
\begin{align*}
    c(x,t+\Delta t) &= c(x,t) + \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} \Delta t + \frac 12 \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial t^2}(\Delta t)^2 + \dots \\
    c(x+\Delta x,t) &= c(x,t) + \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \Delta x + \frac 12 \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \dots \\
    c(x-\Delta x,t) &= c(x,t) - \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \Delta x + \frac 12 \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \dots \\
\end{align*}
We can simplify using $\lambda_r = \lambda_l = \lambda$, then we have 
\begin{align*}
    c + \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} \Delta t + \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial t^2}  \frac {(\Delta t)^2}2 + \dots &= c \\
    &+ \lambda \left(c + \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \Delta x + \frac 12 \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 \right) \\
    &+ \lambda \left(c - \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \Delta x + \frac 12 \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 \right) \\
    &- \lambda c - \lambda c \\
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t}\Delta t + \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial t^2}\frac{(\Delta t)^2}2 &= \lambda \frac{\partial^2c}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 
\end{align*}
Call $\lim_{\Delta x \rightarrow 0, \Delta t \rightarrow 0} \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{\Delta t} \equiv K$ and ignore second order time term as we take the limit.
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} \Delta t &= \lambda (\Delta x)^2\cdot \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2} \\
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= \lambda K\cdot \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}\ \leftarrow \text{Diffusion equation}
\end{align*}
